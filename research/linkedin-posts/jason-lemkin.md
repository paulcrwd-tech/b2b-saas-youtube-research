> In 10 Years of Selling $100M+ of SaaStr Sponsorships ... Zero Reps Who Failed to Learn the Product Closed a Single Deal

Here's a data point from 10 years of selling sponsorships at SaaStr events.

Our reps have averaged about $4M per year in closed revenue. Some did more, some did less. Normal distribution.

But across that entire decade, three reps closed exactly zero. Nothing. Not one deal.

The common thread wasn't laziness. It wasn't lack of hustle. All three worked hard. Made calls. Sent emails. Showed up.

The common thread was that none of them learned the product.

They couldn't answer basic questions. What's the difference between a booth and a sponsorship tier? How many attendees will I get? What worked for similar companies last year? They didn't know. So they defaulted to sending pricing PDFs and hoping.

Buyers sensed it immediately. When a rep can't answer basic product questions on the spot, the buyer's brain does a simple calculation: if this person doesn't understand their own product, how are they going to help me get value from it?

Deal over. Before it started.

This is true at $50K deal sizes and it's true at $500K deal sizes. Product knowledge isn't a nice-to-have skill for sales reps. It's the minimum viable qualification.

And yet. Most sales orgs treat product training as a one-week onboarding exercise and then move on to pipeline reviews and quota attainment discussions forever. 

The reps who keep learning the product after onboarding are the ones who close. The ones who stop learning plateau or wash out.

If you're a sales leader: make product knowledge a continuous requirement, not a checkbox. Quiz your reps. Sit in on their calls. If a rep can't demo the product cold and answer hard questions from a skeptical buyer, they're not ready to carry a quota. No matter how many emails they send.

The hustle matters. The process matters. But none of it works if the rep doesn't know what they're selling.

**Why relevant:** across a decade of $100M+ in sponsorship sales, the reps who closed zero deals (out of an average $4M/rep/year) all shared one trait — lack of deep product knowledge, not lack of effort — reinforcing that product mastery should be a continuous, tested requirement rather than a one-time onboarding checkbox.

>The worst reason of all to vibe code your own CRM is to save money. You won’t, if you include soft costs.

And what happens if the person that built it .. leaves? As they will.

The best reason of all to vibe code your own app?

You simply can’t buy it, and it’s important.

We’ve build now 2+ somewhat autonomous AI Agents, 10K and Qbee, as well as 10+ other agentic apps we rely on for billing, finance, email marketing, ad serving, etc. Our AI VC has also reviewed 4,000+ VC pitch decks and done 1,000,00+ startup valuations.

They are just code. 

But at a practical level, if Amelia or I left SaaStr, no one could maintain them. 

There are full of tribal knowledge, and all built in a way optimized around us.

We build them because we couldn’t buy them.

**Why relevant:**Useful for the playbook as a strategic caveat on the "vibe coding" trend: it reframes the value proposition away from cost savings (which don't materialize once you count soft costs) toward build-vs-buy necessity — SaaStr built 10+ internal AI agents/apps (including an AI VC that's reviewed 4,000+ decks and done 1M+ valuations) only because no off-the-shelf product existed, while flagging the real risk of tribal-knowledge lock-in if the builder ever leaves.
