> SaaS founders think they know their ICP, but when you ask it's always "other founders", "agencies" or "small businesses".

That's not specific enough AT ALL.

Example, FeedHive's main ICP:
Small businesses or founders who are somewhat technical (not developers, but not afraid of automation/APIs) who don't care much about social media, don't consider social media their core marketing channel, but also know they should be somewhat active and would happily pay $29/m to get it out of their way.

It needs to be that specific.

**Why relevant:** A concrete example of how narrow ICP definition should be — critiquing vague answers like "other founders" or "small businesses" in favor of a hyper-specific customer profile (technical-but-not-developer, low social media priority, willing to pay $29/mo to outsource it). Directly useful as a framework/template for defining ICP in the eventual playbook

**Engagement:** [6 Likes, 2 Comments]

>Your SaaS stack usually gets bloated one reasonable decision at a time.

Support gets messy, so you add another helpdesk seat. Reporting gets annoying, so you add another dashboard. Research takes time, so you add another tool. Ops gets scattered, so you add another automation app.

None of these decisions look stupid on their own.

But after a while, the business has 40 tabs, 12 pricing pages, and data spread across too many places.

AI changes the calculation a bit.

You can keep the core stack small:

Database.
Payments.
Support inbox.
Publishing system.
Analytics.

Then let agents do the work around it:

→ read tickets
→ check metrics
→ pull context
→ draft replies
→ find patterns
→ prepare the first version

The founder still approves, rejects, changes direction, and makes the final call.

But you don't need to buy another SaaS product every time a workflow gets annoying

**Why relevant:**A clear framework for avoiding SaaS stack bloat by keeping the core stack minimal (database, payments, support, publishing, analytics) and using AI agents to handle surrounding workflows instead of adding new point-solution tools. Useful as a case study on the "buy vs. build vs. automate" decision founders face when scaling operations.
